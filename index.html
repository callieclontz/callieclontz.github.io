Research

My research focuses on the formation history of the Milky Way's largest and most enigmatic star cluster, Omega Centauri. This complex star cluster stands out against classical globular clusters in many ways and is thought to be the stripped nuclear star cluster of one of our galaxy's most recent and impactful mergers. Understanding its seeding and assembly grants us insights into how galactic centers take shape and gets us closer to unraveling the mystery of the multiple stellar population phenomenon only observed in dense star clusters.
My PhD worked utilized two well-studied probes of nuclear star cluster formation, the age-metallicity relation (AMR) and the Chromosome Diagram (ChD), to study Omega Centauri.

Precise Ages of Individual Stars
In Clontz+ 2024 I constrained precise ($\sigma_{age} > 0.6$ Gyr) ages for $>$8000 individual sub giant branch (SGB) stars using updated isochrone models. In the AMR I uncovered two distinct sequences (black points, upper panel, Fig. 2). However, the tighter, supposed self-enriching, sequence is more metal-poor at a fixed age than the seemingly accreted component, which is inconsistent with our current models of NSC formation (Neumayer+ 2020). The lack of stars with both ages and abundance information makes interpretation difficult. To fill this knowledge gap, I successfully proposed for high-resolution spectroscopic observations (using FLAMES@VLT) of $\sim$120 stars occupying the two streams. I executed the observations in visitor mode in April 2025.

Helium Enhancements as a Function of Metallicity
The ages study focused on the SGB to avoid model complications due to unknown helium abundances, and spectroscopic helium constraints for individual stars are observationally challenging to obtain. Due to these complexities, exact helium abundances for large samples of stars do not exist. Therefore, in my second PhD work (Clontz+ 2025), I focused on the helium enhancement between the P1 (primoridial) population and the P2 (hot-hydrogen-burning polluted, see Fig. 1) populations on the red-giant branch (RGB) in $\omega$ Cen to better constrain its enhancement as a function of metallicity. I built upon previous works by using star-by-star abundances derived via the Data Driven Payne machine learning techniques applied to the MUSE data (Wang+ 2026) as well as ATLAS12 + SYNTHE models to model and subtract contributions to the spread in the ChD from all sources except helium, thus isolating its contribution. The isolated contribution from helium was then converted to an enhancement using a standard ruler. The P2 population exhibits a rapid increase in helium enhancement until [Fe/H] $\sim$-1.9, after which the relationship is flat at $\Delta$ Y = 0.154. This signals a change in the efficiency of helium production and a major shift in the mode of stellar evolution of the P2 population. $\Delta$ Y = 0.15 indicates a total helium mass fraction of Y = 0.40 for the P2 stars which is unprecedented in our Milky Way. The polluter source for such high helium abundances is still unknown and is actively under investigation. This open question has important implication for star formation and evolution in ultra-high stellar density environments and at high redshift.

Individual Stellar Population Parsing
My most recent work (Clontz+ 2026) finally showed the connection between the two assembly probes, the AMR and the ChD, to connect the P1, Im, and P2 populations with the two star formation tracks of the AMR. I began by identifying populations on the RGB, using a Gaussian Mixture model to determine the number of subpopulations, then a KMeans clustering algorithm to apply the first subpopulation labels. I propagated these labels to fainter magnitudes via a K-Nearest-Neighbors algorithm along with luminosity function constraints from our synthetic stellar population modeling. After several iterations to fainter magnitudes, I arrived at subpopulation labels for over 53k stars, across our 14 distinct subpopulations, covering 12.8 $<$ F814W $<$ 19.3. I then matched these with the catalog from my 2024 work, assigning ages to $>$2700 stars. I can now see that the P1, Im, and P2 populations do not map 1-to-1 onto the two-stream AMR (upper panel, Fig. 2) but rather stack vertically in this space. I also compare their respective star formation histories (lower panel, Fig 2.) and see that P1 and Im populations share similar enrichment histories while P2 experienced slower star formation. My recent works, along with the other oMEGACat projects, constitute a large step toward understanding how stellar populations and stellar generations form and co-evolve.

Abundances of SGB Stars: Connecting AMR with Chemistry
My current work is analyzing the spectra of 120 sub-giant branch stars to combine chemical abundances and ages for stars in $\omega$ Cen for the first time. This will allow us to assert the chemical enrichment environments of the two streams of the age-metallicity relation. I proposed, planned, and executed these observations in visitor mode and have also performed all of the data reduction steps myself. Extraction was done with ESOReflex, telluric corrections were done using Molecfit and more precise sky subtraction was applyed using pPXF. I have stacked the 6 exposures I have for each star and am now working on measuring stellar parameters and detailed chemical abundances of $\sim$ a dozen elements using TSFitPy. I have really enjoyed the steep learning curve of doing spectroscopic work from start to finish and am excited to see what I find in the coming weeks and months.
